How to Find Creative Commons and Royalty-Free Music on YouTube

A practical guide to finding Creative Commons and royalty-free tracks on YouTube, verifying licence text in descriptions, keeping clean attribution records and spotting misleading uploads.

Finding background audio on YouTube for a video, podcast, or classroom project often starts with a quick search and ends in confusion. Many uploads claim to be "no copyright" in their titles, yet creators still receive automated claims after publishing. A title is not a licence, and different licences carry very different conditions.

This guide explains how to locate openly licensed audio on YouTube, verify licence text before saving a file, keep attribution records, and spot misleading uploads. YT4MP3 converts YouTube links you supply into MP3 or MP4 for personal, lawful use. That always means respecting creators, copyright law, and YouTube's Terms of Service—working only with your own uploads, Creative Commons or public-domain audio, or tracks you have permission to use.

Creative Commons vs. royalty-free: know the difference

Two common phrases appear across audio searches, and mixing them up leads to broken licence terms.

Creative Commons (CC) is a set of standardised public copyright licences. The creator keeps copyright while granting advance permission to reuse the work under specific rules. On YouTube itself, uploaders can choose only one Creative Commons option: CC BY (Creative Commons Attribution). Under CC BY, you may reuse, edit, and include the track in commercial projects provided you credit the creator, note any changes, and link to the licence.

In video descriptions, composers also link to other CC licences: CC BY-NC (non-commercial only), CC BY-SA (adaptations must share the same licence), CC BY-ND (no modified versions), or CC0 (public-domain dedication without required credit). Failing a condition, such as omitting credit or monetising a CC BY-NC track, ends your permission.

Royalty-free means only that you do not pay ongoing per-play royalties. It does not mean zero restrictions. Many royalty-free channels allow unpaid YouTube use with credit while requiring a paid licence for podcasts, client work, or ads.

How to filter YouTube search by Creative Commons

YouTube provides a search filter for videos tagged with its CC BY flag. It narrows results quickly, though it is only an initial screen rather than legal proof.

  1. Search by musical attributes. Enter the mood, instrument, or tempo you want—for instance, acoustic guitar underscore, ambient synth pad, or upbeat marimba loop.
  2. Open Filters. On desktop, click Filters near the top-right of the results list. On mobile, tap the filter icon or search filters menu.
  3. Choose Creative Commons. Under Features, select Creative Commons so results show only uploads with the CC BY metadata flag.
  4. Inspect each video separately. Open the watch page and read the description instead of trusting a playlist title.
  5. Check the uploader. Visit the channel's About page to confirm whether the uploader is the composer, a label, or an archive.

Keep in mind that the Creative Commons filter relies on a checkbox selected by the uploader. YouTube does not audit every flagged video to verify ownership.

Using the YouTube Audio Library

For most creators, teachers, and podcasters, the safest starting point is YouTube's built-in Audio Library inside YouTube Studio. Tracks carry documented terms and an official download button directly on YouTube.

  1. Open YouTube Studio. Sign in with your Google account, click your profile avatar, and choose YouTube Studio. You do not need published videos to browse it.
  2. Open Audio library. Select Audio library in the left sidebar.
  3. Pick Music or Sound effects. Switch tabs depending on whether you need full tracks or short transitions and ambient effects.
  4. Filter by project needs. Narrow results by Genre, Mood, Instrument, Duration, or Attribution (Attribution not required vs. Attribution required).
  5. Read the Licence type details. Hover over or click the badge in the Licence type column. Attribution-required tracks display the exact credit text to paste into your project.
  6. Use the built-in Download button. Hover over a row and select Download to save the 320 kbps MP3 from Studio.

Check platform scope before publishing elsewhere. The standard Audio Library licence covers videos and projects you create while forbidding standalone redistribution of the audio file. If you publish an audio podcast outside YouTube, review each track's pop-up wording carefully and prefer tracks marked CC BY 4.0 when portable cross-platform clarity matters.

How to check licence text in video descriptions

When you locate a track through public YouTube search, check the full description from top to bottom before using it.

  1. Expand the description. Click ...more below the player so lower metadata fields appear.
  2. Check the Licence line at the bottom. A genuine CC upload reads Licence: Creative Commons Attribution licence (reuse allowed) near the bottom. If that line is absent or lists the standard YouTube licence, YouTube's CC BY toggle is not active.
  3. Look for a Content ID "Music" block. Above the licence line, YouTube often shows a Music card listing Song, Artist, and Licensed to YouTube by. When a commercial distributor or rights agency appears there, Content ID is tracking the audio; do not treat it as openly reusable without written permission from that rights holder.
  4. Verify external licence links. Some musicians keep the standard YouTube licence while linking to a CC BY 4.0 or CC BY-NC 4.0 page in the description. Compare both pages and follow the stricter rule if they conflict.

Red flags that a track is not really free to reuse

Mislabelled uploads are common on large platforms. Look elsewhere if you spot any of these warning signs:

  • Mismatched uploader and well-known audio. An unverified compilation channel posting a film score, game soundtrack, or pop instrumental under CC BY does not hold the rights to grant that licence.
  • "No copyright" in the title, standard licence below. Many promo channels use catchy titles while keeping the standard YouTube licence and linking to paid licensing stores in the description.
  • Content ID music credits at the bottom. A "Licensed to YouTube by" entry from a distributor or collecting society signals automated claims on public uploads.
  • Missing composer or provenance details. Descriptions containing only hashtags leave no way to confirm who owns the master and composition.
  • "DMCA-safe" promises without written terms. Informal assurances can change if an artist later joins a distributor and enables Content ID retroactively.
  • Old compositions in modern recordings. A classical piece from the 1800s has a public-domain score, yet a recent orchestral recording carries its own recording copyright.
  • Restricted streams. Never save or reuse audio from paid, rented, protected, live, or private streams.

Rules for podcasts, videos and school projects

Your publishing format determines which licences work safely.

YouTube and social videos

Ad revenue, sponsorships, affiliate links, and business promotion count as commercial use. Avoid CC BY-NC tracks on monetised channels, even if ads are disabled today and you plan to monetise later. Choose CC BY, CC0, or YouTube Audio Library tracks, and put attribution near the top of your description.

Podcasts and audio feeds

Podcasts travel via RSS feeds outside YouTube and often carry ads or listener support. Confirm that commercial use is permitted (skip NC if you earn revenue) and that the licence is not restricted to YouTube videos. Standard CC BY 4.0 and CC0 tracks work cleanly across podcast feeds. Place full credits in episode show notes and add a brief spoken credit in the outro if feeds truncate text.

School, university and classroom projects

Closed classroom assignments are non-commercial, so CC BY-NC tracks usually fit alongside CC BY and CC0. Still, student projects often end up in public portfolios or festivals later. Selecting CC BY or CC0 early avoids re-editing later, and a closing credit slide keeps citations tidy.

How to keep attribution records and a simple template

Channels rename, descriptions change, and videos are occasionally deleted. Keeping a compact attribution log next to your project files preserves proof of the licence you checked.

Save a spreadsheet or text file in your project's audio folder with five fields for every track:

  • Track title and creator name exactly as written by the author.
  • Exact source URL (the YouTube watch URL or Studio Audio Library track title and ID).
  • Licence name and version (such as CC BY 4.0, CC BY-NC 4.0, CC0, or YouTube Audio Library).
  • Date checked (YYYY-MM-DD) plus a copied snippet or screenshot of the licence notice.
  • Modifications made (for example, trimmed to 35 seconds, faded out, lowered under speech).

Creative Commons recommends the TASL format—Title, Author, Source, and Licence—plus any changes:

Attribution template: "[Track Title]" by [Creator Name] ([Source URL]), licensed under [Licence Name and Version] ([Licence Deed URL]). Modifications: [describe edits, or write "none"].

Example entry: "Sample Acoustic Loop" by Creator Name (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=example123), licensed under CC BY 4.0 (https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/). Modifications: trimmed to 0:42 and lowered volume under voiceover.

Never phrase a credit to suggest the artist endorses your work. When preparing audio from your own uploads or verified CC BY sources you are entitled to use, pick sensible export settings. Frequently asked questions

Does "royalty-free" mean I never have to credit the artist?

No. Royalty-free means no ongoing per-play royalties. Many royalty-free tracks still require visible credit in descriptions or show notes, and some restrict commercial use without a paid tier.

If a video appears under YouTube's Creative Commons filter, can I reuse its audio anywhere?

Not automatically. The filter reflects an uploader checkbox, not a legal check by YouTube. Always confirm the uploader owns the track, inspect the description's Licence line, and check for Content ID music credits.

Can I use CC BY-NC music in a monetised video or sponsored podcast?

No. NC stands for Non-Commercial. Monetised channels, sponsored episodes, and business marketing videos count as commercial use, so choose CC BY, CC0, or a commercial licence instead.

What if a track I credited later gets a copyright claim?

Check your attribution log for the date, URL, and licence text you saved. If the rights holder released it under CC BY, that record supports a dispute; if a third party mislabelled someone else's audio, replace the track via YouTube Studio.

Where should I put music attribution in a video, podcast, or slideshow?

Place it where audiences can see it easily: near the top of your YouTube description, inside podcast show notes, or on a closing credits slide in a presentation.
