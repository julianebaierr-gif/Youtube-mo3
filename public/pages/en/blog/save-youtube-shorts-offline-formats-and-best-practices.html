Saving YouTube Shorts Offline: Formats, Ratio and Good Practice

Shorts are vertical, short and easy to lose track of. See which format fits, how to copy a link, how much storage to plan for, and how to keep your use lawful and respectful of creators.

YouTube Shorts are short vertical videos, and they behave differently from the widescreen clips most of us grew up with. They are framed for a phone held upright, they loop, and they are often only a few seconds long. That makes them easy to watch and a little awkward to keep, edit or show on a television. This guide covers what a Short technically is, which file format suits which purpose, how to copy a Shorts link on a phone and on a computer, how much storage to plan for, and how to play vertical video on a big screen or in an editor.

It also covers the part that matters most: whose video you are saving. Offline copies are fine for your own work and for material you have permission to keep. They are not a licence to collect or repost what other people made. The sections below keep that distinction in view from start to finish.

What YouTube Shorts actually are

A Short is a regular YouTube video that is uploaded in a vertical or square frame and falls within the maximum length YouTube allows for the format. That maximum has changed over time. It started at 60 seconds and was later extended to up to 3 minutes, so you will see both short and fairly long clips under the Shorts label. Check YouTube's current help pages if the exact limit matters for something you plan to publish.

The standard frame is 9:16, meaning the picture is nine units wide for every sixteen tall. On a phone this fills the whole screen. Square (1:1) and 4:5 videos are also accepted, but 9:16 is what the Shorts feed is designed around. A Short is not a separate kind of file. Under the hood it is the same family of video and audio streams used for any other upload, which is why the same formats and conversion options apply.

Before you save anything: rights and permission

A Short that is free to watch is not automatically free to keep, copy or republish. Copyright belongs to the creator unless they have chosen otherwise, and YouTube's Terms of Service set limits on how its content may be accessed. A tool that converts a link you provide does not change either of those facts. The responsibility for using it lawfully stays with you.

A sensible rule of thumb is to save a Short offline only in these situations:

  • It is your own upload and you want a backup or a copy for editing.
  • It is published under a Creative Commons licence or is in the public domain, and you follow the licence terms such as attribution.
  • The creator has given you clear permission, ideally in writing, for the purpose you have in mind.
  • Your local law permits a private copy for personal use and the content is not paid, rented, protected, live or private.

Do not save paid or rented content, protected streams, live broadcasts or private videos, and do not re-upload someone else's Short as your own. If you want the longer explanation of fair use, licences and what each one permits, read YouTube copyright, fair use and Creative Commons. You can also see how this site handles rights questions in the DMCA policy.

MP4 or MP3: choosing the right format

The right file type depends on what you want to do with the clip afterwards. Shorts are mostly visual, but some are really audio with a loose background, such as a spoken tip, a voice memo or a piece of your own music.

FormatWhat it keepsBest forTrade-off
MP4 (video)Picture and sound in one fileWatching offline, sharing your own clip, editingLarger files
MP3 (audio)Sound onlyListening to your own spoken or musical content, reviewing a voice trackNo picture at all

MP4 is the safer default because it plays on nearly every phone, computer, tablet and television without extra software. Choose MP3 only when the sound alone is what you need. Keep in mind that a Short's audio is often short and compressed already, so converting it to a high MP3 bitrate will not add detail that was never there. How to copy a Shorts link on mobile and desktop

Every conversion starts with the video's address. A Shorts link looks like youtube.com/shorts/ followed by an eleven-character video ID. Here is how to copy it in each situation.

On the YouTube mobile app

  1. Open the Short you want, ideally your own or one you have permission to use.
  2. Tap the Share arrow on the right-hand side of the screen.
  3. Choose Copy link from the sharing sheet.
  4. Open the converter in your phone's browser and paste the link into the input box.

In a mobile browser

  1. Open the Short in your browser.
  2. Tap the address bar and select the whole address.
  3. Choose Copy, then paste it into the converter page.

On a desktop or laptop

  1. Open the Short in your browser. The address in the bar will contain /shorts/.
  2. Click the address bar, press Ctrl+A (or Cmd+A on a Mac) and then Ctrl+C (or Cmd+C).
  3. Alternatively, click Share under the video and choose Copy.
  4. Paste the link into the tool.

If the link opens but the converter does not accept it, check that the address is complete and has no extra text before or after it. Links to private, age-restricted or live content will not work, and that is the intended behaviour. For a phone-focused walkthrough, the iPhone and Android guide shows the process step by step.

Storage and organisation

Short clips look tiny, but they add up. A folder of 200 clips at 1080p, averaging 30 seconds each, can reach roughly 1.5 to 3 GB depending on motion and bitrate. That is manageable on a laptop, yet noticeable on a phone with limited space.

  • Pick 720p when the clip will only be watched on a phone. It looks fine on a small screen and uses far less space.
  • Keep 1080p only for your own clips that you may edit later, and only when the source offers it.
  • Use a consistent naming pattern, for example 2026-05-12_topic_creator-name.mp4, so you can find files and remember where each came from.
  • Store a short text note beside any file that is not yours, recording the creator, the link and the permission or licence.
  • Back up your own clips on a second drive or cloud folder, and delete temporary copies you no longer need.

Watching vertical video on a TV

A 9:16 clip on a 16:9 television leaves wide black bars on both sides, because the picture occupies only about one third of the screen width. That is normal, not a fault in the file. You have a few sensible options.

  • Accept the bars. For a quick family viewing of your own clips this is the simplest route. Most media players on TVs and streaming sticks will show the video centred.
  • Cast from the phone. Casting or screen mirroring shows the vertical video exactly as it appears on the phone. The result is similar, with bars on the sides.
  • Use a blurred-background version. In an editor, place the vertical clip over a scaled and blurred copy of itself to fill the frame. Only do this with your own footage or with permission.
  • Rotate the display. A computer monitor on a rotating stand can be turned to portrait mode, which suits vertical video well.

Format support varies by television. If a file will not play, MP4 with the common H.264 video codec and AAC audio is the most widely accepted combination. Copy the file to a USB stick formatted as FAT32 or exFAT, or play it from a computer connected by HDMI.

Using Shorts in a video editor

Editing your own Shorts, for example to combine several into a compilation or to make a widescreen version, works best when you start from the right project settings.

  • Create the project at 1080 x 1920 and 30 fps (or match the source frame rate) if the output will stay vertical.
  • Create a 1920 x 1080 project when the output is for a TV or a standard YouTube video, then scale and position the vertical clip inside it.
  • Avoid stretching. Changing a 9:16 clip to fill a 16:9 frame without cropping will distort faces and text.
  • Keep titles and captions inside the central safe area, because app interfaces cover the bottom and right edges on phones.
  • Export as MP4 with H.264 for upload compatibility.

Whenever you need music for a project, choose tracks that are cleared for your use. The article on finding Creative Commons and royalty-free music on YouTube explains what to look for and how to record attribution.

Credit the creator when you share

Sharing is where most problems begin. If a clip is yours, share it however you like. If it belongs to someone else, a link is almost always better than a file. Linking sends views and credit to the person who made it, and it lets them remove or update the video later.

When you do have permission, or the material is licensed for reuse, follow these habits:

  • Name the creator and link to the original Short.
  • State the licence, such as CC BY, and include the version if the licence requires it.
  • Do not crop out names, handles or captions that identify the creator.
  • Do not present a clip as your own, and do not re-upload unlicensed work to another channel or platform.
  • If a creator asks you to delete a copy, do so promptly.

A quick checklist before you convert

  • Is this my own Short, a licensed one, or one I have permission to keep?
  • Is it public, and neither paid, rented, protected nor live?
  • Do I need video (MP4) or only audio (MP3)?
  • Is 720p enough, or do I truly need more, and does the source offer it?
  • Have I noted the creator and licence somewhere I can find later?

Frequently asked questions

How long can a YouTube Short be?

YouTube currently allows Shorts of up to 3 minutes, having started at 60 seconds. Because platform rules change, check YouTube's help documentation for the present limit before you plan a project around it.

What aspect ratio should I use for Shorts?

Use 9:16, typically 1080 x 1920 pixels. Square and 4:5 videos can also appear in the feed, but vertical 9:16 fills the screen and is the format the feed is designed around.

Should I save a Short as MP4 or MP3?

Save MP4 if you want the picture and sound together, which is best for playback and editing. Choose MP3 only when the audio alone matters, such as your own spoken or musical content. MP3 cannot hold any video.

Can I save any Short I find?

No. Save only your own Shorts, material under a Creative Commons licence or in the public domain, or content you have clear permission to keep. Respect copyright and YouTube's Terms of Service, and never re-upload other people's work.

Why does my vertical video have black bars on the TV?

A 9:16 picture is much narrower than a 16:9 screen, so the unused space on the sides is filled with black. The file is not broken. You can cast it, accept the bars, or make your own blurred-background version in an editor.
