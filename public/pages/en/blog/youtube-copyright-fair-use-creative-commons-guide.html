YouTube Copyright, Fair Use and Creative Commons Explained

A plain-English guide to copyright basics, YouTube's Terms of Service, Creative Commons licences, public domain and fair use, with practical steps for using only content you own or have permission to use.

Almost everyone who watches YouTube eventually wants a copy of something for offline use: a lecture before a flight, a family clip, a soundtrack for a school project. Whether that is acceptable depends less on the tool you use and more on who owns the material, what licence applies and what permission you hold. This guide explains the basics in plain language so you can make careful decisions. It is educational information, not legal advice, and the rules differ between countries.

Copyright basics in plain language

Copyright is an automatic right that belongs to the creator of an original work, such as a video, a song, a script or a photograph. In most countries it arises the moment the work is fixed in some form. Nobody has to register it or add a copyright symbol. A phone video recorded this morning is already protected, and so is a professional music video.

The owner normally controls copying, distribution, public performance and adaptation. A single video can contain several layers of rights: the footage itself, the music beneath it, the script, and sometimes artwork or clips from other creators. Each layer can have a different owner, which is why a video that looks simple can be complicated to reuse.

Copyright lasts for a long time, commonly the life of the author plus 50 to 70 years depending on the country. That is why recent uploads are almost always protected, and why genuinely free-to-use material usually announces itself through a licence or a public-domain statement.

What YouTube's Terms of Service say

Separate from copyright law, YouTube has its own rules for using its service. Its Terms of Service generally say that you may view content through the service as offered and that you should not access, reproduce or distribute content by other means unless YouTube provides a download feature or you have written permission from YouTube or the rights holder. Features such as Premium downloads exist inside the official apps for exactly this reason.

In practice this means two checks, not one. Even when a video carries a permissive licence, read the current terms for the service and ask whether your use is covered. Terms change over time, so rely on the live text on YouTube rather than on a summary, including this one. If you are unsure, the safe options are the official download feature, content you own, or a direct agreement with the creator.

Creative Commons licences explained

Creative Commons (CC) is a set of standard public licences that let creators grant advance permission for certain uses. The creator still owns the work. The licence simply states what everyone else may do and on which conditions. Each licence is built from a few elements: BY (credit the creator), SA (share adaptations under the same licence), NC (non-commercial use only) and ND (no derivatives, so no edits).

CC BYCredit the creator, link to the licence, note changesReuse and remix, including commercial projects
CC BY-SACredit, and share adaptations under the same licenceCollaborative projects
CC BY-NCCredit, no commercial useClassroom, hobby and community projects
CC BY-NDCredit, no modified versionsSharing a work unchanged
CC BY-NC-SA / BY-NC-NDCombinations of the conditions aboveNon-commercial sharing with extra limits
CC0Creator waives rights as far as the law allowsUnrestricted reuse without credit being required

Two points are often missed. First, a licence is not a loophole: if you break the conditions, such as leaving out credit, the permission can fall away. Second, NC means you should not use the work in anything that earns money, which includes monetised channels and many promotional videos.

How to recognise Creative Commons on YouTube

YouTube supports only one Creative Commons option for uploaders, the CC BY licence. Videos under it are labelled in the description area. Here is a careful way to check one:

  1. Open the video page and expand the description by selecting more.
  2. Look near the bottom for a line reading Licence: Creative Commons Attribution licence (reuse allowed). The standard YouTube licence is shown as a different label.
  3. When searching, use the Filters menu and choose Creative Commons under Features to narrow results.
  4. Check that the uploader really owns the material. A reposted film clip cannot be licensed by someone who does not hold the rights, even if the label says CC BY.
  5. Note the creator's name, video title and link so that you can give proper credit later.

Be aware that the CC BY label on YouTube covers the video as uploaded. Music inside it may belong to someone else, so listen for third-party tracks and read the description for credits. Public domain works

A work enters the public domain when its copyright has expired, when it was never eligible for protection, or when the creator has deliberately dedicated it to the public. Examples include old recordings whose term has run out, some government publications in certain countries, and material released under CC0.

Public domain status is national. Something free of copyright in one country can still be protected in another, and a new recording or restoration of an old work can carry fresh rights even if the underlying composition is public domain. A 1920s symphony may be free to use while last year's orchestral performance of it is not. Look for an explicit statement from the uploader or an archive, and keep a note of where you found it.

Your own uploads and original work

The simplest lawful case is material you made yourself. If you filmed, recorded and edited a video, you generally hold the rights, apart from any third-party music or footage inside it. Typical examples are class presentations, tutorials, band rehearsals, family events and personal vlogs.

Keeping local copies of your work is good practice, because platforms can remove videos, accounts can be locked and original project files get lost. Getting permission

When a video is neither yours nor openly licensed, the lawful route is to ask. A short, polite message is usually enough to start. Good practice looks like this:

  • Contact the creator through the email or business address on their channel's About page.
  • State exactly what you want to do, for example play the audio in a school presentation, and for how long.
  • Say whether the use is private, educational or commercial.
  • Keep the reply. A saved email is your evidence if a question arises later.
  • Respect a refusal or silence. No answer is not a yes.

Permission can be narrow. A creator who agrees to a classroom screening has not agreed to a public upload. Stay within the scope you were given.

Fair use and fair dealing vary by country

Many laws include exceptions that allow limited use of protected works without permission. In the United States this is called fair use, judged case by case on four factors: the purpose and character of the use, the nature of the work, how much was used, and the effect on the market for the original. In the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia and other places, fair dealing applies instead, with a closed list of purposes such as criticism, review, news reporting, quotation, research and education.

These exceptions are narrow, depend on the facts and are decided by courts, not by a rule of thumb. Common myths are unreliable: using only 30 seconds, giving credit, or saying the use is non-profit does not automatically make a use lawful. Some countries also allow private copying of lawfully obtained material, others do not, and some restrict it for streamed content. Check the rules where you live, and consider speaking to a qualified lawyer for anything high stakes.

Attribution done properly

Attribution is a legal condition of CC BY and a courtesy everywhere else. A good credit line is easy to write and should include the title, creator, source and licence. For example:

"Title of Video" by Creator Name, YouTube link, licensed under CC BY.

Place it where viewers can see it, such as the slide footer, the video description or the end of a document. If you changed the work, say so, for example "trimmed to 40 seconds". Do not suggest the creator endorses your project unless they said so.

What responsible users do

  • Check the source first. Confirm the licence or ownership before you convert anything.
  • Leave out restricted material. Do not save paid, rented, protected, live or private content, because the creator and platform have not made it available for copying.
  • Keep it personal. Use files for private offline viewing, study or your own projects, and do not re-upload other people's work.
  • Keep records. Store the licence text, permission email and source link next to the file.
  • Support creators. Watch on the platform when you can, subscribe, and buy or licence work you value.
  • Take problems seriously. If a creator asks you to stop using their material, stop.

Frequently asked questions

Is it legal to save a YouTube video for personal use?

It depends on the country, the content and YouTube's terms. Your own uploads and material with a suitable licence or permission are the clearest cases. Laws on private copying differ widely, so check local rules and do not assume that personal use is automatically allowed.

Does the Creative Commons label mean I can do anything with the video?

No. CC BY requires credit and compliance with its conditions, and the uploader must actually hold the rights. Music or clips inside the video may belong to other people and may not be covered by the label.

Can I use a short clip under fair use?

Possibly, but only a court can decide. Fair use and fair dealing depend on purpose, amount and effect on the original. Length alone is not a safe test, and the exceptions differ between countries.

Is something old automatically in the public domain?

Not necessarily. Terms vary by country and a recent recording or edit of an old work can have its own rights. Look for an explicit public-domain statement from a reliable source.

What should I do if a creator asks me to remove their content?

Stop using it and delete your copies of their work. Respecting the request is the right thing to do and keeps the relationship with creators healthy.
